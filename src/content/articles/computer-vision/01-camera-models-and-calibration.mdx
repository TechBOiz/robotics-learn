Before a robot can use an image to act, it must answer a geometric question: *which direction in space does this pixel correspond to?* A learned policy may absorb the answer implicitly, but any system that measures, grasps or localises with a camera needs it explicitly. That answer is the **camera model**, and finding its parameters is **calibration**.

## The pinhole model

<Figure number={1} caption="The pinhole model. The image plane is drawn in front of the optical centre, which is mathematically equivalent to the real sensor behind it and avoids an upside-down image.">
  <Pinhole />
</Figure>

Take a point at $(X, Y, Z)$ in the camera's own frame, with $Z$ pointing out along the optical axis. By similar triangles it lands at pixel

$$
u = f_x\,\frac{X}{Z} + c_x \qquad v = f_y\,\frac{Y}{Z} + c_y
$$

Four numbers describe an ideal camera:

- $f_x, f_y$ — the focal length, measured in pixels. A longer focal length magnifies and narrows the view.
- $c_x, c_y$ — the *principal point*, where the optical axis meets the image. It is near the image centre, but not exactly at it.

They are collected in the **intrinsic matrix**:

$$
K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
Z\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = K \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \end{bmatrix}
$$

<Callout title="Division by Z is the whole problem">
Projection divides by depth, so every point along a ray lands on the same pixel. A small object nearby and a large one far away look identical. Going backwards, a pixel gives a *direction*, not a position. Recovering the missing depth is what [stereo, structured light and LiDAR](/computer-vision/seeing-depth/) are for.
</Callout>

### Field of view

The horizontal field of view follows from focal length and image width $w$:

$$
\text{FoV} = 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{w}{2 f_x}\right)
$$

A wide lens sees more of the scene and makes tracking through fast motion easier. A narrow lens puts more pixels on each object, which improves measurement accuracy at a distance. Robots often carry both.

## Lens distortion

Real lenses bend straight lines, most strongly toward the image edges. The standard correction models **radial** distortion as a polynomial in the distance $r$ from the image centre:

$$
x_{\text{distorted}} = x\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6)
$$

plus a smaller **tangential** term for a lens that is not perfectly parallel to the sensor. Here $x$ is the ideal coordinate before multiplying by $K$.

Once the coefficients are known, an image can be *undistorted* so that the pinhole equations hold. Very wide and fisheye lenses need a different model, because the polynomial breaks down beyond about 120° of view.

## Extrinsics: where the camera is

Intrinsics describe the camera itself. **Extrinsics** are the [rigid transform](/dynamics/frames-and-transforms/) from some other frame to the camera frame:

$$
{}^{C}p = {}^{C}T_{W}\;{}^{W}p
$$

Putting it together, a point in the world reaches a pixel by: transform into the camera frame, divide by depth, distort, apply $K$.

## Calibrating intrinsics

The usual method, due to [Zhang](https://doi.org/10.1109/34.888718), needs only a flat printed pattern:

1. Show the camera a checkerboard of known square size from fifteen or more different angles and distances.
2. Detect the corners in each image, to sub-pixel precision.
3. Solve for the intrinsics, distortion coefficients and the board's pose in every image, by minimising **reprojection error** — the distance between where the model says each corner should appear and where it was detected.

Practical points:

- **Cover the whole image**, especially the corners. Distortion is only observable where there is data.
- **Tilt the board.** Images taken square-on cannot separate focal length from distance.
- **Check the result.** An RMS reprojection error of a few tenths of a pixel is typical of a good calibration. A low error on a poor set of images still gives a poor calibration.
- **Recalibrate after changing focus or zoom.** Both alter the intrinsics.

Libraries such as [OpenCV](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/dc/dbb/tutorial_py_calibration.html) implement the whole procedure.

## Hand–eye calibration

For a robot, the transform that matters is between the camera and the robot's own frames. There are two arrangements:

- **Eye-in-hand:** the camera rides on the wrist. Unknown: camera relative to the tool flange.
- **Eye-to-hand:** the camera is fixed in the workcell. Unknown: camera relative to the robot base.

Both are found the same way. Move the arm through a series of poses while the camera views a fixed target (or a target on the arm). Each pair of poses gives one equation of the form

$$
A\,X = X\,B
$$

where $A$ is the arm's motion from its encoders, $B$ is the camera's motion from the target, and $X$ is the unknown mounting transform. Enough varied poses — with rotations about at least two different axes — determine $X$.

Hand–eye error is usually the largest term in a vision-guided robot's accuracy budget. A one-degree error in camera orientation is a 17 mm error at one metre.

## Things the pinhole model leaves out

- **Rolling shutter.** Most low-cost sensors expose one row at a time. During fast motion each row is captured from a slightly different pose, which skews the image. Global-shutter sensors expose all rows together and are preferred on fast-moving robots.
- **Motion blur.** Exposure time trades brightness against sharpness. Short exposures need good lighting.
- **Timing.** An image is only useful if you know *when* it was taken relative to the joint encoders and the [IMU](/sensors/inertial-measurement-units/). A 20 ms error at 1 m/s is 20 mm.
- **Depth of field and focus.** Close-range manipulation may push objects out of focus.
