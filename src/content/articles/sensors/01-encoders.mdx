A robot that does not know its joint angles cannot do anything else. Position sensing is the most basic measurement on the machine, and it does double duty: the drive needs rotor angle to [commutate the motor](/actuators/motor-drive-algorithms/), and the controller needs joint angle to know where the limb is.

## Incremental and absolute

An **incremental encoder** reports *changes* in angle. A disc with evenly spaced lines passes a detector, producing two pulse trains a quarter-cycle apart.

<Figure number={1} caption="Quadrature output. The quarter-cycle offset between A and B is what encodes direction.">
  <Quadrature />
</Figure>

Counting every edge on both channels gives four counts per line, so a 1,000-line disc resolves 4,000 positions per turn. It is simple and fast. Its weakness is that it has no memory: at power-up the count is zero wherever the shaft happens to be, and the robot must move to a known reference to find itself. A third channel, the *index*, pulses once per turn for this purpose.

An **absolute encoder** reports the angle itself, immediately at power-up. For a robot that may be switched on with its arm raised over a workpiece, or mid-stride, that matters. Almost all modern robot joints use absolute sensing.

**Multi-turn** absolute encoders also track how many full revolutions have occurred. This is needed on the motor side of a reducer, where the rotor turns many times per joint revolution. They keep count through power loss with a small battery, an energy-harvesting pulse wire, or a geared second stage.

## How the angle is sensed

| Principle | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Optical** | Light through or off a patterned disc | Highest resolution and accuracy | Sensitive to dust, oil and shock |
| **Magnetic, on-axis** | Chip reads the field of a magnet on the shaft end | Small, cheap, robust | Needs the shaft end; affected by stray fields |
| **Magnetic, off-axis** | Sensors read a magnetised ring | Leaves the centre open for a hollow shaft | Lower accuracy without calibration |
| **Inductive** | Coils on a board sense a patterned metal target | Immune to magnetic fields and contamination; hollow-shaft friendly | Larger; needs careful mounting |
| **Capacitive** | Patterned electrodes on rotor and stator | Thin, low power | Sensitive to moisture and gap changes |
| **Resolver** | A rotating transformer giving sine and cosine signals | Extremely rugged; no electronics on the shaft | Bulky; needs conversion electronics |

<Figure number={2} caption="The most common arrangement on small robot actuators: a magnet on the rotor shaft and a sensor chip on the drive board.">
  <MagEncoder />
</Figure>

The on-axis magnetic encoder dominates in compact actuators because the sensor chip can sit directly on the drive's circuit board. Its drawback is that it occupies the centre of the shaft — exactly where a hollow-shaft joint wants to route cables. Those joints use off-axis magnetic or inductive rings instead.

## Resolution, accuracy, repeatability

These three are often confused.

- **Resolution** is the smallest step the sensor reports. An $n$-bit encoder divides a turn into $2^n$ steps.
- **Accuracy** is how far the reported angle can be from the true angle.
- **Repeatability** is how consistently it reports the same angle for the same true position.

| Bits | Steps per turn | Angle per step |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 4,096 | 0.088° |
| 14 | 16,384 | 0.022° |
| 17 | 131,072 | 0.0027° |
| 19 | 524,288 | 0.0007° |

A sensor can have fine resolution and poor accuracy. Magnetic encoders in particular have errors that repeat once or twice per turn, caused by the magnet being slightly off-centre or tilted. Because the error repeats, it can be measured once and corrected with a lookup table — good repeatability is what makes calibration possible.

At the end of a 0.5 m arm, one step of a 14-bit encoder on the joint is about 0.2 mm of motion at the tip. Placed on the motor side of a 100:1 reducer, the same encoder resolves a hundred times finer at the joint — but then it no longer sees the reducer's backlash and flex.

## Velocity is harder than position

No common sensor measures joint velocity directly. It is computed from position:

$$
\omega \approx \frac{\theta_k - \theta_{k-1}}{\Delta t}
$$

Position comes in steps, so this estimate comes in steps too, of size (angle per step) / (sample period). For a 14-bit encoder sampled at 10 kHz:

$$
\frac{0.022^\circ}{0.0001\ \text{s}} = 220^\circ/\text{s}
$$

<Callout title="The quantisation trap">
At 10 kHz, a 14-bit encoder cannot distinguish zero velocity from 220°/s in a single sample. Raw differenced velocity is far too noisy to feed into a damping gain. Sampling faster makes it worse, not better.
</Callout>

Practical drives therefore estimate velocity with a filter or an observer — often a tracking loop that models the shaft as moving at a smoothly varying speed and corrects itself from each new angle reading. That trades noise for a small delay, and the delay limits how much damping gain a joint controller can use. Higher encoder resolution directly relaxes this trade, which is one reason high-performance joints use 17 bits or more.

## Why two encoders per joint

Many robot joints carry one encoder on the motor and another on the output.

- **The motor-side encoder** gives the rotor angle for commutation, at high effective resolution, with no reducer dynamics in between.
- **The output-side encoder** gives the true joint angle, including backlash and reducer flex, and gives it absolutely at power-up.

Together they offer something more: the *difference* between them, scaled by the gear ratio, is the twist in the reducer. Since the reducer behaves like a stiff spring, that twist is a rough measure of joint torque — the same principle as a [series elastic actuator](/actuators/designing-the-alternatives/), without an added spring.

## Interfaces

Incremental encoders output raw A/B/index pulses. Absolute encoders use a serial link: SPI for chips on the same board, and differential protocols such as SSI, BiSS-C or EnDat for cabled sensors. For control, what matters is that the reading is taken at a known instant, synchronised to the drive's control cycle. A position sample of uncertain age is a source of velocity error.
