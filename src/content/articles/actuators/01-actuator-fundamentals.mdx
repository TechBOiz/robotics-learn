A robot's controller can only ever ask for one thing: *push this joint this hard*. The **actuator** is the part that makes it happen. It takes energy from a battery, a pump or a compressor and turns it into force on a link.

Everything a robot can physically do — how fast it moves, how much it lifts, whether it can catch itself when it trips, whether it is safe to stand next to — is bounded by its actuators. That is why actuator design is where most arguments about robot hardware end up.

## The chain from energy to motion

"Actuator" usually means the whole chain, not just the motor:

1. **Energy source** — a battery, a hydraulic pump, an air supply.
2. **Drive** — power electronics or valves that meter energy on command. For an electric motor this is an inverter running a current control loop.
3. **Motor** — the element that converts energy into mechanical force: an electric motor, a hydraulic cylinder, a pneumatic muscle.
4. **Transmission** — gears, screws, belts or cables that trade speed for force.
5. **Sensors** — at minimum position; often current, torque and temperature.

A design choice anywhere in this chain shows up in the others. A motor that produces more torque needs less gearing; less gearing changes what the sensors can see; and so on. Read an actuator as a system.

## Torque, speed and power

For a rotary joint the three basic quantities are torque $\tau$ (N·m), angular speed $\omega$ (rad/s) and mechanical power:

$$
P = \tau\,\omega
$$

For a linear actuator, swap in force $F$ and velocity $v$: $P = F\,v$.

Power is the budget. A transmission can exchange torque for speed, but it cannot create power — it only loses a little to friction.

### Where an electric motor's limits come from

Most robots are driven by permanent-magnet electric motors, and two relationships govern them. Torque is proportional to current:

$$
\tau = K_t\, I
$$

and the voltage needed rises with speed, because a spinning motor generates its own opposing voltage (back-EMF):

$$
V = I R + K_e\,\omega
$$

These two lines produce the shape in Figure 1.

- **The speed limit is electrical.** Once back-EMF approaches the supply voltage there is no voltage left to push current, so torque falls to zero at the *no-load speed*.
- **The torque limit is thermal.** Current through winding resistance makes heat, $P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R$. Since torque is proportional to current, *heat grows with the square of torque*. Double the torque, four times the heat.

That second point is why every motor has two torque ratings. **Continuous torque** is what it can hold indefinitely without overheating. **Peak torque** is several times higher but only available for seconds, until the windings get hot.

## What a gearbox really does

A motor on its own is fast and weak relative to what a joint needs. A reducer with ratio $N$ fixes that:

$$
\tau_{\text{out}} = N\,\eta\,\tau_m \qquad \omega_{\text{out}} = \frac{\omega_m}{N}
$$

where $\eta$ is the transmission efficiency. Torque goes up by $N$, speed down by $N$.

The cost is less obvious, and it is the single most important idea in this series. Seen from the output, the motor's rotor appears $N^2$ times heavier:

$$
J_{\text{reflected}} = N^2 J_m
$$

A rotor that feels like nothing in your hand, behind a 100:1 gearbox, behaves like something **10,000 times** its inertia when the link is pushed from outside. Friction in the motor and gears is multiplied the same way.

In practice this means a high-ratio joint:

- **hits hard** — in a collision, that reflected inertia has to be stopped by the gear teeth,
- **hides forces** — an external push is absorbed by friction before it reaches the motor, so motor current stops being a good estimate of joint torque,
- **resists being moved** — it is not *backdrivable*.

Low gear ratios avoid all three, but need a bigger motor to make the same torque. That tension — torque density against transparency — is what separates the actuator architectures in the [next article](/actuators/types-of-actuators/).

## The six numbers

When you read an actuator datasheet or a paper, these are the quantities to look for.

| Metric | What it tells you | Typical unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Torque / force** | How hard it can push, continuously and at peak | N·m, N |
| **Speed** | How fast the output moves at no load and under load | rad/s, rpm, m/s |
| **Torque density** | Torque per unit mass — the key figure for anything that carries its own actuators | N·m/kg |
| **Power density** | Power per unit mass — what limits explosive motion like jumping | W/kg |
| **Bandwidth** | How quickly force or position can be changed — sets how stiff and how responsive control can be | Hz |
| **Backdrivability** | How easily the output can be moved from outside — governs impact tolerance, safety and force sensing | reflected inertia, friction torque |

Three more appear once you look closer:

- **Efficiency** — how much input power becomes mechanical output. For a mobile robot it is run time.
- **Stiffness and backlash** — how much the output deflects under load, and how much free play there is when direction reverses. These bound positioning accuracy.
- **Motor constant** $K_m = K_t / \sqrt{R}$ — torque per square-root-watt of heat. It is the fairest way to compare two motors, because unlike $K_t$ it cannot be changed just by rewinding with different wire.

<Callout title="Caution" tone="caution">
Torque density figures are only comparable when they describe the same thing. Check whether a quoted number is peak or continuous, and whether the mass includes the gearbox, housing, drive electronics and cooling. A motor-only peak figure and a whole-module continuous figure can differ by an order of magnitude for the same hardware.
</Callout>

## No actuator wins on everything

Each of the six numbers can be improved, but not all at once:

- More gearing raises torque density and lowers backdrivability and efficiency.
- A larger-diameter motor raises torque without gearing, at the cost of mass and packaging.
- Adding a spring protects the gearbox and gives clean force sensing, and lowers bandwidth.
- Hydraulics deliver extreme force density at the joint, and move the mass, noise and losses to a pump.

Robot actuator design is choosing which of these to give up for a particular job. An industrial arm that must repeat a weld to a fraction of a millimetre makes a different choice from a quadruped that must survive landing a jump — and the rest of this series is about those choices.
