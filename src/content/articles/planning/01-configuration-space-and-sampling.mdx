"Move the arm from here to there without hitting anything" sounds simple and is one of the harder problems in robotics. The arm is a complicated shape, it bends in many places at once, and the obstacles are arbitrary. The idea that makes it manageable is a change of viewpoint.

## Configuration space

A **configuration** is a complete specification of where every part of the robot is. For an arm it is the vector of joint angles $q$. The set of all configurations is the **configuration space**, or C-space. Its dimension is the number of degrees of freedom.

In C-space the robot is a single point. Obstacles become regions: the set of all configurations in which some part of the robot would intersect something.

Three things are visible in Figure 1:

- A simple obstacle in the workspace becomes a large, irregular region in C-space. There is no formula for its shape.
- Start and goal may be near each other in the workspace and far apart in C-space, or the reverse.
- Planning is now a clean question: find a continuous curve from one point to another through **free space**.

## Why not just compute the free space?

For two joints we can draw the picture. A typical arm has six or seven, and a humanoid has thirty or more. A grid with 100 values per joint has $100^7 = 10^{14}$ cells for a seven-joint arm. This is the *curse of dimensionality*, and it rules out any method that enumerates C-space.

What we *can* do cheaply is answer a point query: **is this one configuration in collision?** Run [forward kinematics](/dynamics/kinematics-and-the-jacobian/), place the robot's geometry, and test it against the environment. Sampling-based planners are built entirely on that one operation.

## Probabilistic roadmaps

A **probabilistic roadmap** (PRM) builds a reusable graph of the free space:

1. Sample many random configurations. Keep those that are collision-free.
2. Try to connect each to its nearest neighbours with a straight segment in C-space. Keep the segment if every point checked along it is collision-free.
3. To answer a query, connect the start and goal to the graph and run a shortest-path search.

Building the roadmap is expensive; each query afterwards is fast. PRM suits a fixed environment with many queries, such as a robot cell whose layout never changes.

## Rapidly-exploring random trees

A **rapidly-exploring random tree** (RRT) answers a single query by growing a tree from the start:

1. Sample a random configuration $q_{\text{rand}}$.
2. Find the tree node nearest to it, $q_{\text{near}}$.
3. Take a short step from $q_{\text{near}}$ toward $q_{\text{rand}}$. If that step is collision-free, add the new node.
4. Repeat until a node lands near the goal.

The tree spreads quickly because a random sample is most likely to fall in a large unexplored region, and so is most likely to be nearest to a node on the frontier. The tree is pulled outward toward the unexplored space without ever being told where that is.

Common refinements:

- **Goal bias.** Sample the goal itself a small fraction of the time.
- **RRT-Connect.** Grow two trees, one from each end, and try to join them. Usually much faster.
- **RRT\*.** After adding a node, rewire nearby nodes through it if that shortens their path. The tree then improves the longer it runs.

## What the guarantees mean

Sampling-based planners give weaker promises than a grid search, and it is worth being exact about them.

- **Probabilistic completeness.** If a path exists, the probability of finding it approaches one as the number of samples grows. With finite time, failure does not prove that no path exists.
- **Asymptotic optimality.** Plain RRT and PRM find *a* path, usually a poor one. Variants such as RRT\* and PRM\* [converge to the shortest path](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1186) as samples increase. Plain RRT provably does not.

<Callout title="Narrow passages">
Sampling finds wide-open regions easily and thin gaps rarely. Reaching into a shelf, threading through a doorway, or a tight insertion each corresponds to a narrow corridor in C-space that random samples seldom hit. This is the classic weakness of these methods, and where planning times become unpredictable.
</Callout>

## From a path to a motion

The raw output is a jagged sequence of configurations, as Figure 2 shows. Before a robot can execute it:

1. **Shortcut.** Repeatedly pick two points on the path and replace the section between them with a straight segment, if that segment is collision-free.
2. **Smooth.** Fit a curve through the result.
3. **Time-parameterise.** Assign velocities and accelerations within the robot's limits. See [trajectory optimisation](/planning/trajectory-optimisation/).

## In practice

- **Collision checking dominates the run time.** Robots are approximated by simple shapes — spheres, capsules, convex hulls — and environments are stored in structures that reject distant geometry cheaply.
- **Distance needs a definition.** "Nearest" in C-space mixes joints of different kinds and sizes. A weighted metric is normally used.
- **Constraints complicate sampling.** Keeping a cup upright restricts valid configurations to a thin surface in C-space, which random samples never land on exactly. Planners project samples onto it.
- **Libraries exist.** The [Open Motion Planning Library](https://ompl.kavrakilab.org/) implements these algorithms and many variants, and is what MoveIt uses underneath.

Sampling-based planning excels at finding *some* feasible path through cluttered, high-dimensional space with no initial guess. It is weaker at producing smooth, efficient, dynamically aware motion. That is the strength of optimisation, and the two are commonly chained: sample to find a path, then optimise it.
