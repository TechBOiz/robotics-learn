A robot never knows its state. It has a model that predicts roughly what should happen, and sensors that report roughly what did. **State estimation** is the discipline of combining them, and the **Kalman filter** is its central tool. Sixty years after it was introduced, it or one of its descendants runs inside nearly every robot, drone and phone.

## Carry a belief, not a number

The key move is to stop tracking a single best guess and instead track a **probability distribution**: a best guess together with how uncertain it is. The Kalman filter uses the simplest useful distribution, a Gaussian, described completely by a mean $\hat x$ and a covariance $P$.

Two operations then alternate.

## One dimension

Suppose we track a single quantity — a position along a line.

**Predict.** We believe the position is $\hat x$ with variance $P$. The robot is commanded to move by $u$, and that motion is itself uncertain, with variance $Q$:

$$
\hat x^- = \hat x + u \qquad P^- = P + Q
$$

The estimate shifts and its variance grows. Moving without looking always makes you less sure.

**Update.** A sensor now reports position $z$, with noise variance $R$. Combine the two, weighting each by its confidence:

$$
K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}
\qquad
\hat x = \hat x^- + K\,(z - \hat x^-)
\qquad
P = (1 - K)\,P^-
$$

<Figure number={2} caption="An update. The result is a weighted average of prediction and measurement, and is more certain than either.">
  <KalmanStep />
</Figure>

Everything about the filter can be read from the **Kalman gain** $K$:

- If the sensor is poor ($R$ large), $K \to 0$: ignore the measurement and keep the prediction.
- If the prediction is poor ($P^-$ large), $K \to 1$: jump to the measurement.
- In between, it is a weighted average, with weights set by the two variances.

And $P$ always shrinks in an update. Any measurement, however noisy, adds information.

## The general form

For a state vector $x$ with linear dynamics and linear measurements,

$$
x_k = A\,x_{k-1} + B\,u_k + w_k
\qquad
z_k = H\,x_k + v_k
$$

where $w$ and $v$ are noise with covariances $Q$ and $R$, the same two steps become:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{Predict:}\quad & \hat x^- = A\,\hat x + B\,u \\
& P^- = A\,P\,A^{\mathsf T} + Q \\[4pt]
\textbf{Update:}\quad & K = P^- H^{\mathsf T}\,(H\,P^- H^{\mathsf T} + R)^{-1} \\
& \hat x = \hat x^- + K\,(z - H\,\hat x^-) \\
& P = (I - K\,H)\,P^-
\end{aligned}
$$

The term $z - H\hat x^-$ is the **innovation**: the part of the measurement the filter did not predict.

<Callout title="It estimates things it cannot measure">
The matrix $H$ may observe only part of the state. Measure position alone, and the filter still estimates velocity — because the dynamics $A$ link them, and the covariance $P$ records that link. This is how an [IMU bias](/sensors/inertial-measurement-units/) gets estimated from occasional position fixes, with no sensor ever measuring bias.
</Callout>

## Tuning: what Q and R mean

The filter is optimal only if $Q$ and $R$ are right, and in practice they are the tuning knobs.

- **$R$, measurement noise**, can often be measured: hold the robot still and compute the variance of the sensor's output.
- **$Q$, process noise**, represents everything the model leaves out — unmodelled forces, slip, discretisation. It is rarely known and is usually tuned.

What matters is their ratio. Raise $Q$ relative to $R$ and the filter trusts sensors more: it responds quickly and passes through more noise. Lower it and the filter trusts the model more: smooth, but slow to notice real change.

A useful check is the innovation itself. If the filter is well tuned, innovations should be zero-mean, uncorrelated over time, and of the size the filter expects. Innovations that are consistently larger mean the filter is overconfident.

## Nonlinear systems

Real robots are nonlinear: rotations, [camera projection](/computer-vision/camera-models-and-calibration/), range measurements. Three extensions handle that.

| Filter | Idea | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Extended (EKF)** | Linearise the model about the current estimate at every step | Cheap and standard; can diverge if the estimate is far off or the nonlinearity is strong |
| **Unscented (UKF)** | Push a small set of sample points through the true nonlinear model | No derivatives needed; often more accurate; a little more computation |
| **Particle filter** | Represent the belief with many random samples | Handles any distribution, including several hypotheses at once; cost grows quickly with state dimension |

Orientation needs particular care. Rotations do not add like vectors, so a filter cannot simply average two [quaternions](/dynamics/frames-and-transforms/). The standard remedy is an **error-state** filter: keep the full orientation as a quaternion, and let the filter estimate only a small three-parameter correction to it.

## Where it breaks

- **Outliers.** A Gaussian has thin tails, so one wildly wrong measurement pulls the estimate hard. Real systems reject measurements whose innovation is implausibly large before using them.
- **Overconfidence.** If $P$ becomes too small the filter stops listening to its sensors. Linearisation error in an EKF pushes it in exactly this direction.
- **Unobservable states.** Some quantities cannot be inferred from the available sensors — [yaw from an IMU alone](/sensors/inertial-measurement-units/), for example. The filter will still produce a number, with a covariance that grows without bound. Check the covariance, not just the estimate.
- **Wrong timing.** The filter assumes each measurement describes the present. A delayed measurement applied as if it were current is a systematic error.

## What comes next

A filter keeps only the latest state and discards history. That is efficient, but once it linearises around a wrong estimate, the mistake is baked in. The alternative is to keep past states and re-optimise them all as new data arrives. That is **smoothing**, and it is the basis of modern [SLAM](/state-estimation/slam/).
