Motors move joints. Tasks are specified at the hand, the foot or the camera. **Kinematics** is the geometry that connects the two, with no forces involved yet. Almost everything can be seen in the simplest useful robot: two links in a plane.

## Forward kinematics

<Figure number={1} caption="A two-link planar arm. One set of joint angles gives one tip position; one tip position can come from two sets of joint angles.">
  <TwoLinkArm />
</Figure>

Given joint angles, where is the tip? Add the two links as vectors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= l_1\cos\theta_1 + l_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\
y &= l_1\sin\theta_1 + l_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Forward kinematics always has exactly one answer, and for any serial robot it is a product of [transforms](/dynamics/frames-and-transforms/), one per joint. It is cheap and never fails.

## Inverse kinematics

Given a tip position, what are the joint angles? Squaring and adding the two equations eliminates $\theta_1$:

$$
\cos\theta_2 = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2}{2\,l_1 l_2}
$$

Then

$$
\theta_2 = \pm\arccos(\cdot)
\qquad
\theta_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(l_2\sin\theta_2,\; l_1 + l_2\cos\theta_2\big)
$$

Three things appear here that are true of inverse kinematics in general:

- **Multiple solutions.** The $\pm$ gives elbow-up and elbow-down. A six-joint industrial arm typically has up to eight.
- **No solution.** If the right-hand side exceeds 1 in magnitude, the target is out of reach.
- **Infinitely many solutions** once a robot has more joints than the task needs — a seven-joint arm can move its elbow while holding its hand still.

Closed-form solutions exist only for particular geometries. Everything else is solved numerically, using the Jacobian.

## The Jacobian

Differentiate forward kinematics with respect to time. The result is linear in the joint velocities:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\dot x \\ \dot y\end{bmatrix}
= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
-l_1 s_1 - l_2 s_{12} & -l_2 s_{12} \\
\phantom{-}l_1 c_1 + l_2 c_{12} & \phantom{-}l_2 c_{12}
\end{bmatrix}}_{J(\theta)}
\begin{bmatrix}\dot\theta_1 \\ \dot\theta_2\end{bmatrix}
$$

with $s_1 = \sin\theta_1$, $c_{12} = \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ and so on. In general:

$$
\dot x = J(q)\,\dot q
$$

Each column of $J$ is the velocity the tip would have if only that joint moved at unit speed. The matrix depends on the pose, so it must be recomputed as the robot moves.

### The same matrix maps forces

Power is the same whether measured at the joints or at the tip: $\tau^{\mathsf T}\dot q = F^{\mathsf T}\dot x = F^{\mathsf T}J\dot q$. Since this holds for any $\dot q$:

$$
\tau = J(q)^{\mathsf T} F
$$

<Callout title="Why this matters so much">
The Jacobian transpose converts a force you want at the hand or foot into the joint torques that produce it, with no matrix inversion. It is the basis of force control, impedance control and legged locomotion, and it is why [torque-controlled actuators](/actuators/types-of-actuators/) are so useful.
</Callout>

## Singularities

For the two-link arm the determinant of the Jacobian is strikingly simple:

$$
\det J = l_1 l_2 \sin\theta_2
$$

It is zero when $\theta_2 = 0$ or $\pi$: arm fully straight or folded back on itself. There the two columns of $J$ point the same way, and the tip cannot move along the direction of the arm at all.

<Figure number={2} caption="The set of tip velocities reachable with unit joint speed. Near a singularity it flattens toward a line.">
  <Manipulability />
</Figure>

Near a singularity:

- **Velocity:** moving the tip in the lost direction demands enormous joint speeds. An inverse-kinematics solver that is not protected will command them.
- **Force:** by the transpose relation, the arm can resist huge forces along that direction with almost no joint torque. Standing with straight legs exploits exactly this.

A common scalar measure of distance from singularity is the **manipulability** $w = \sqrt{\det(J J^{\mathsf T})}$, the volume of the ellipse in Figure 2.

## Numerical inverse kinematics

For a general robot, iterate. With the current error $e = x_{\text{target}} - x(q)$, take a step

$$
\Delta q = J^{\mathsf T}\big(J J^{\mathsf T} + \lambda^2 I\big)^{-1} e
$$

and repeat. This is **damped least squares**. The damping $\lambda$ is what keeps the step bounded near singularities, at the price of slightly slower convergence. With $\lambda = 0$ it reduces to the pseudo-inverse, which blows up exactly where Figure 2 shows the ellipse collapsing.

When the robot has spare joints, the extra freedom can be used without disturbing the task. Any joint motion of the form

$$
\dot q = J^{+}\dot x + (I - J^{+}J)\,\dot q_0
$$

achieves the task velocity $\dot x$ while the second term, projected into the Jacobian's null space, pursues a secondary goal $\dot q_0$ such as staying away from joint limits.

## What changes for real robots

- **Six dimensions.** The task is a full pose, so $J$ has six rows: three for linear and three for angular velocity.
- **Describing the geometry.** Link frames are specified by a URDF file or similar; the older Denavit–Hartenberg convention packs each joint into four parameters.
- **Joint limits and collisions.** A mathematically valid solution may be physically unreachable. Practical solvers treat inverse kinematics as constrained optimisation.
- **Legs.** For a foot on the ground the same Jacobian runs the other way: it turns joint motion into body motion, and joint torques into ground reaction force.
