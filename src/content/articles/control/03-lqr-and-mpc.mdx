[PID](/control/pid-control/) asks you to choose gains. **Optimal control** asks you to state what you want — small error, little effort, no limit violated — and derives the controller. That shift matters most for systems with many coupled states, where there is no intuition for what the gains should be.

## The problem

Write the system in discrete time with state $x$ and input $u$:

$$
x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k)
$$

and choose inputs to minimise a cost summed over time:

$$
J = \sum_{k} \Big( x_k^{\mathsf T} Q\,x_k + u_k^{\mathsf T} R\,u_k \Big)
$$

Here $x$ is measured from the target, so the first term penalises error and the second penalises effort. The matrices $Q$ and $R$ are the tuning knobs. They are easier to reason about than gains: "I care ten times more about tilt than about position" is a statement about $Q$.

## LQR

If the dynamics are linear, $x_{k+1} = A\,x_k + B\,u_k$, and there are no limits on anything, the problem has an exact solution. The optimal input is a constant gain on the state:

$$
u_k = -K\,x_k \qquad K = (R + B^{\mathsf T}P\,B)^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}P\,A
$$

where $P$ solves the discrete algebraic Riccati equation

$$
P = Q + A^{\mathsf T}P\,A - A^{\mathsf T}P\,B\,(R + B^{\mathsf T}P\,B)^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}P\,A
$$

This is the **linear-quadratic regulator**. Every numerical library can solve it. What it gives:

- **All gains at once.** For a system with $n$ states and $m$ inputs, $K$ has $n \times m$ entries, each chosen consistently. Balancing an inverted pendulum on a cart by hand-tuning is tedious; with LQR it is one function call.
- **Guaranteed stability** of the linear model, with good robustness margins.
- **A meaning for $P$.** The quantity $x^{\mathsf T}P\,x$ is the total future cost from state $x$ — the *cost-to-go*. This idea returns in MPC and in reinforcement learning, where it is called the value function.

Its limits are the two assumptions. Real robots are nonlinear, so LQR is applied around an operating point or along a trajectory by linearising there. And real robots have limits — torque, joint range, friction — which LQR ignores entirely. It will ask for whatever input the gain dictates.

## Model-predictive control

**Model-predictive control** (MPC) handles constraints directly. At every control step it solves an optimisation over a finite window into the future:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{u_0,\dots,u_{N-1}} \quad & \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ell(x_k,u_k) + \ell_N(x_N) \\
\text{subject to}\quad & x_{k+1} = f(x_k,u_k), \quad x_0 = x_{\text{measured}} \\
& u_{\min}\le u_k\le u_{\max}, \quad g(x_k,u_k)\le 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Then it applies only the first input, discards the rest, and solves again from the next measurement.

<Callout title="Why throw the plan away?">
The plan was computed from a model, and the model is wrong in small ways. Acting on only the first step, then re-planning from what actually happened, corrects for that error continuously. The optimisation gives foresight; the repetition gives feedback.
</Callout>

What MPC adds over LQR:

- **Constraints are respected.** If a motor will saturate in 200 ms, the plan accounts for it now.
- **Preview.** A known future — a step coming up, a reference trajectory that turns — shapes the current action.
- **Changing models.** The dynamics can differ at each step of the horizon, such as which feet are on the ground.

What it costs:

- **Computation.** An optimisation must finish within one control period, every period.
- **Model dependence.** It is only as good as $f$.
- **Tuning** moves to the cost weights, horizon length and terminal cost $\ell_N$. The terminal cost stands in for everything beyond the horizon; a good choice, often the LQR cost-to-go, lets the horizon be short.

### Making it fast enough

When the dynamics are linear and the cost quadratic, the problem is a **quadratic program** (QP) — convex, with a single optimum that solvers find reliably in milliseconds. Much practical MPC design is the art of simplifying the model until the problem is a QP.

For nonlinear problems, common approaches are to linearise around the previous solution and solve a QP at each step, or to run only one or two iterations of a nonlinear solver per cycle, starting from the last plan shifted forward by one step. That *warm start* is essential.

## MPC on legged robots

A legged robot's full dynamics are nonlinear, and [its body can be moved only by contact forces](/dynamics/equations-of-motion/) that must stay inside friction cones. That is a constrained optimisation by nature.

An influential formulation, used on the [MIT Cheetah 3](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/138000), makes it tractable with three simplifications:

1. Model the robot as **a single rigid body** pushed by forces at the feet, ignoring leg mass.
2. Fix the **footstep timing and locations** in advance, so that which feet are in contact at each step of the horizon is known.
3. Assume small roll and pitch, so the rotational dynamics become linear.

With these, choosing ground reaction forces over the horizon is a QP, with friction cones as linear constraints, solved in a few milliseconds.

The result is a two-layer controller:

- **MPC**, at tens of hertz, chooses contact forces over the next half second or so using the simplified model.
- **Whole-body control**, at around a kilohertz, solves a smaller QP using the full dynamics to find joint torques that realise those forces while tracking swing-foot and posture targets.

## MPC and learned policies

A policy trained by reinforcement learning can be seen as doing MPC's job with the optimisation moved offline. Training searches, in simulation, for a function that maps state directly to a good action. At run time that function is evaluated in microseconds, with no solver.

| | Model-predictive control | Learned policy |
|---|---|---|
| **Computation at run time** | Solves an optimisation each step | One network evaluation |
| **Constraints** | Explicit, enforced | Encouraged through reward; not guaranteed |
| **Model** | Simplified so it can be solved online | Full simulator, used offline |
| **New task** | Change the cost; works immediately | Retrain |
| **Unmodelled effects** | Limited by the simplified model | Can absorb them if simulated or randomised |

The two are increasingly combined: a learned policy for robust low-level behaviour, with an optimisation layer above it for planning and constraints.
