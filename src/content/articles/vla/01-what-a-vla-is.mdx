A policy trained by [behaviour cloning](/machine-learning/imitation-and-reinforcement-learning/) on one task learns that task, in that kitchen, with those objects. Move the cup or change the lighting and it may fail. The limiting factor is data: no robot dataset is remotely large enough to cover the variety of the real world.

Models trained on the internet's images and text, on the other hand, already know what a cup is, what "left of the sink" means, and what a tidy table looks like. A **vision-language-action model** (VLA) is an attempt to borrow that knowledge for robot control.

## The idea

Start with a pretrained **vision-language model** (VLM): a network that takes images and text and produces text. Fine-tune it on robot demonstrations so that, given camera images and an instruction, it produces *actions*.

The term comes from [RT-2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15818) in 2023, which made the step in the most direct way possible. It wrote each action as a short string of numbers, treated those as ordinary text tokens, and trained the VLM to emit them. Trained jointly on robot data and on web-scale vision-language tasks, the model followed instructions involving objects and concepts that never appeared in its robot data — for example, moving an item onto a particular number or picking the smallest object.

That result is the core claim of the approach:

<Callout title="What the VLM contributes">
Semantic knowledge transfers. The robot data teaches *how to move*; the internet pretraining supplies *what things are and what the words mean*. The robot data therefore need not contain every object and phrasing the robot will meet.
</Callout>

## The parts

**Vision encoder.** Converts each camera image into a sequence of feature vectors. [OpenVLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09246), for example, combines two pretrained encoders, DINOv2 and SigLIP, one stronger on spatial detail and one on meaning.

**Language model backbone.** A transformer that processes the image features and the instruction tokens together. Sizes range from a few hundred million parameters to several billion.

**Action head.** The stage that turns the backbone's output into numbers the robot can execute. This is where designs differ most, and it is covered in [the next article](/vla/actions-data-and-the-field/). The main choices are discrete tokens, as in RT-2, or a diffusion or flow-matching head that generates continuous actions, as in [π0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24164).

**Action chunk.** Nearly all current models output a short sequence of future actions at once — commonly around a second's worth — instead of a single step.

**Robot state.** Joint angles and gripper position, usually fed in alongside the images.

## Two speeds

A multi-billion-parameter model cannot run at the rate a robot arm needs. A large VLM takes on the order of a hundred milliseconds per pass; smooth, reactive control wants commands many times faster than that.

The common answer is to split the model in two.

  <DualSystem />
</Figure>

Figure describes its [Helix](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix) model this way: a 7-billion-parameter vision-language model running at 7–9 Hz, feeding an 80-million-parameter network that outputs control for 35 degrees of freedom of the upper body at 200 Hz, all on the robot's on-board GPUs. NVIDIA's [GR00T N1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14734) uses the same division, with a vision-language "System 2" and a diffusion-transformer "System 1". The names borrow from psychology's distinction between deliberate and automatic thinking.

The figures above are the developers' own. The design principle is the durable part: slow semantic reasoning and fast motor control are separate problems, and they run at separate rates.

## How one is trained

1. **Pretrain the VLM** on internet images and text. This is usually done by someone else; the VLA starts from a published model.
2. **Pretrain on robot data from many sources.** Demonstrations from different robots, labs and tasks are pooled. The [Open X-Embodiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08864) collection, assembled across many institutions, made this practical for academic groups; OpenVLA was trained on 970,000 demonstrations drawn from it.
3. **Co-train on non-robot tasks** such as image captioning and visual question answering, so the semantic knowledge is not forgotten while the model learns to act.
4. **Fine-tune** for a specific robot and task, often with a comparatively small number of demonstrations.

Step 2 depends on **cross-embodiment** training: a single model learning from robots with different arms, grippers and camera placements. Each robot's actions are mapped into a shared format, and the model is told which robot it is controlling. A model that has seen many bodies adapts to a new one with less data than a model trained from scratch.

## What they can do

As of this writing, published demonstrations include folding laundry, clearing tables, loading dishwashers, packing boxes and sorting groceries, driven by natural-language instructions. The most significant recent claim is about generalisation to new places: Physical Intelligence reported that its [π0.5](https://www.pi.website/blog/pi05) model completed long household tasks in homes that were entirely absent from its training data.

## What remains hard

- **Reliability.** Success rates that look impressive in a demonstration video are far below what unattended industrial use requires. A 90% success rate on a ten-step task fails most of the time.
- **Speed.** Most demonstrations run well below human pace.
- **Precision and force.** Tasks needing tight tolerances or controlled force — insertion, fastening — are much harder than pick-and-place. Most VLAs see only images and joint angles, with no [sense of touch](/sensors/force-torque-and-touch/).
- **Long horizons.** Errors accumulate over many steps, and the model must remember what it has already done.
- **Evaluation.** There is no agreed benchmark on real hardware. Results from different groups, on different robots, are not comparable.
- **Data.** Robot demonstrations remain expensive, and how much is enough is unknown.
- **Safety.** A model that will attempt any instruction needs constraints applied outside the model. See [safety standards](/safety/).

## Where a VLA sits in the stack

A VLA replaces the task-level and motion-level reasoning: what to do, and roughly how to move. It does not replace what lies beneath. Its action chunk is a set of targets for the [joint-level controller](/control/impedance-control/), which still runs at kilohertz rates on [actuators](/actuators/) that must be able to follow it. For a humanoid, a separate learned or model-based controller usually handles balance and walking, and the VLA commands the arms and hands on top of it.
